    Part 5: Adaptive Sampling

    Adaptive Sampling

    In standard Monte Carlo path tracing, we usually shoot a fixed number of samples per pixel. However, this is computationally inefficient. Some pixels in a scene converge to their true color very quickly. Other pixels are "complex," such as areas with soft shadows, or glossy reflections, and they need a lot of samples to remove noise.

    Adaptive sampling is a technique that solves this problem. Instead of using a fixed number of samples for every pixel, we dynamically check if a pixel has converged while we are rendering it. If the pixel's color has stabilized, we stop sampling early for that pixel.

    To implement adaptive sampling, we use online statistics to track the mean and variance of the samples as we collect them:

    • \(s_1=\sum_{k=1}^n\limits L_k\): to accumulate the scalar illuminance of each sample.
    • \(s_2=\sum_{k=1}^n\limits L_k^2\): to accumulate the squared scalar illuminance of each sample.

    Checking for convergence after every single sample is too expensive. Instead, I check once every samplesPerBatch. During the check, I calculate the mean \(\mu\) and the variance \(\sigma^2\) of the samples using the following formulas:

    \[ \mu = \frac{s_1}{n}, \quad \sigma^2 = \frac{1}{n-1}\left(s_2 - \frac{s_1^2}{n}\right) \]

    Next, we calculate \(I=1.96 \cdot \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\), which represents the half-width of the \(95\%\) confidence interval. According to the Central Limit Theorem, when the sample size is large enough, the sample mean follows a normal distribution. The value \(1.96\) is the z-score that covers \(95\%\) of the standard normal distribution.

    If \(I\) is sufficiently small compared to the mean, we can confidently say the pixel has converged. The condition for early stopping is: \(I \leq \text{maxTolerance} \cdot \mu\). If this is true, we break out of the sampling loop early.

    Render Results

    The resulting sampling rate images clearly visualize how adaptive sampling dynamically distributes computational effort across the scene. In both images, the directly illuminated walls appear blue, meaning they converged very quickly and required only a small fraction of the maximum samples. Conversely, the complex regions—such as the soft shadows beneath the bunny and the spheres—appear red and green. This confirms that the algorithm successfully identified these difficult areas and concentrated the sampling rays there to eliminate noise.
